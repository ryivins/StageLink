# Reflection

## Decisions

One of the biggest decisions I made during this project was choosing to build a visually focused website instead of a more text-heavy or technical one. Since StageLink is centered around live events, production, and stage design, I wanted the site to immediately communicate energy and professionalism through visuals, layout, and motion. I decided to prioritize strong sections like services, galleries, testimonials, and event categories because those are the areas users would most likely care about first.

Another important decision was keeping the navigation simple and predictable. I avoided overly complicated menus or interactions because I wanted visitors to move through the site naturally without confusion. I also chose a modern dark-themed aesthetic with strong contrast and large imagery because it fit the entertainment and production industry better than a minimalist corporate style would have.

I also made the decision to focus heavily on responsive design early instead of treating mobile support as an afterthought. A lot of event-related browsing happens on phones, so I spent time making sure sections stacked properly, text stayed readable, and images scaled correctly across screen sizes.

Finally, I chose to keep the scope realistic. There were ideas I considered adding, like advanced booking systems or backend functionality, but I realized that trying to build everything at once would reduce the overall quality of the project. Instead, I focused on polishing the front-end experience and making the site feel complete and professional.

## What Worked

The visual design worked better than I expected. The strongest part of the project is probably the overall presentation and branding consistency. The combination of sections, colors, spacing, and imagery helped the site feel like a real business website instead of just a class project. I am especially satisfied with the gallery and services sections because they showcase the company clearly while also making the page feel dynamic.

The responsiveness also turned out well. Early in development I had problems with spacing and alignment on smaller screens, but after reorganizing layouts with flexbox and responsive sizing, the site became much cleaner across devices. Seeing the same design adapt correctly on desktop and mobile felt rewarding because it made the site feel more production-ready.

I’m also happy with how much more comfortable I became with structuring pages. At the beginning, I tended to place elements randomly and fix problems later. During this project I started thinking more intentionally about hierarchy, section flow, and how users visually move through a page.

Another thing that worked well was learning through experimentation. Instead of stopping whenever I hit a problem, I spent more time testing different CSS properties, layouts, and design approaches until I found something that worked. That process made me more independent as a developer.

## What I Would Do Differently

If I restarted the project, I would spend more time planning before writing code. Early on, I changed layouts multiple times because I started building before fully deciding how the sections should connect together. Creating wireframes or a clearer structure first would have saved time and reduced unnecessary rewrites.

I would also organize my code better from the beginning. As the project grew, some of my CSS became repetitive and harder to manage. Breaking styles into clearer sections or reusable components earlier would have made future edits easier.

Another thing I would improve is accessibility. While I focused heavily on visuals, I did not spend enough time thinking about accessibility features like semantic HTML structure, contrast testing, keyboard navigation, or screen-reader friendliness. If I continued developing the project, I would make accessibility a bigger priority.

I also would have liked to include more interactivity. Some subtle animations or transitions were added, but I think the experience could feel even more immersive with better motion design and more dynamic content.

## What I Learned

This project taught me that web development is not just about making something functional — it is also about creating a clear experience for users. Design choices, spacing, color palettes, and responsiveness all affect how professional and trustworthy a website feels.

I learned a lot about CSS layout systems, especially flexbox and responsive design principles. Before this project, I understood the basics, but I often struggled to make layouts behave consistently across different screen sizes. Working through those problems improved my confidence significantly.

I also learned that debugging is a major part of development. Many issues came from small mistakes, like conflicting styles or incorrect positioning, and solving them required patience and attention to detail. Over time I became better at isolating problems and testing solutions instead of randomly changing code.

On a personal level, I learned that I tend to aim for perfection too early. Sometimes I spent too long adjusting small visual details instead of focusing on overall progress. I got better at balancing quality with momentum and understanding when something was “good enough” to move forward.

Most importantly, I learned that building for the web is highly iterative. The final version of the site looks very different from the original idea I had at the start. Each revision improved the project, and that process showed me that strong web development comes from constant refinement rather than getting everything correct immediately.
